A screen model for taking the test (layout of question, answer, whiteboard and progress, plus the navigation model) Open ↗

Each question is one page of a paper workbook: question, answer boxes and a squared working space on the same page; progress is 'Page 5 of 36'; the facts section is a drill sheet · Benefit: Matches the brand (paper workbooks) and the books the child gets next; the working space is always in view, so showing work feels normal · Cost: Less room for big answer targets; the page is busy for a grade 1 child · Risk: 'Page 5 of 36' may feel long; a paper look on a screen can feel fake

B screen model for taking the test (layout of question, answer, whiteboard and progress, plus the navigation model) Open ↗

Question and answer on the left, a full-height whiteboard on the right on every question; progress is 36 segments with a dot for the halfway break · Benefit: The most room for shown work, so the best work images for the report and the AI review; good with a stylus · Cost: Half the screen is a whiteboard even on questions that do not need it · Risk: Children may scribble on the board all the time, and the empty board may distract

C screen model for taking the test (layout of question, answer, whiteboard and progress, plus the navigation model) Open ↗

One huge question and four large coloured answer tiles, nothing else; the whiteboard is a full-screen sheet, and on a show-your-work question it opens as step 2 after the answer; 'read every question' option · Benefit: Lowest load for ages 6-8; the largest touch targets; the read-aloud is the first thing on the screen · Cost: Shown work is a separate step, so it takes longer on the 7 work questions · Risk: Children skip optional work because the board is hidden; the AI gets fewer work images

D screen model for taking the test (layout of question, answer, whiteboard and progress, plus the navigation model) Open ↗

Progress is the main picture: a trail with 36 stops, a halfway camp (break and facts) and a finish flag; the question is a card; the whiteboard is a drawer that opens by itself on show-your-work questions · Benefit: Most motivating over 30-45 minutes; the break and the facts section have a place in the story · Cost: The trail takes height; the number pad becomes one row of keys to fit · Risk: Reads as a game; 36 stops in view can make the test look long; less like the paper brand

E screen model for taking the test (layout of question, answer, whiteboard and progress, plus the navigation model) Open ↗

The layout children know from school online tests (FSA, EQAO): a tool rail (read aloud, whiteboard, bigger text), a question map, Back and Next; the child can skip and come back within a part, and the end of a part lists what is not finished · Benefit: Lets the child go back and skip (the research found this helps younger children); familiar from grade 4 on; the bigger-text tool · Cost: Dense; the most chrome; the map of 18 squares is a lot for a grade 1 child · Risk: Feels like an exam, which raises stress; going back interacts with the two-stage routing (allowed only inside a part here)
