### 1.2 Evidence by product

**i-Ready Diagnostic**
- Vendor page: computer-adaptive, K-12 reading and math, "specific test flows, stopping rules"; test flow for math varies by grade band; reports scale score, placement level ("above grade level" through "two or more levels below"), percentile rank, Quantile measure, and "detailed information on student performance by domain". Norms: "more than five million students" for post-pandemic national norms. Source: https://www.curriculumassociates.com/reviews/assessment/science-behind-the-diagnostic
- The vendor page does not state test length or item count. State-approved list gives i-Ready "K-1st (25-35 min.), grades 2-6 (40-75 min.)". Source: https://educate.iowa.gov/media/11538/download (2026-27 Approved Early Mathematics Assessments, Iowa).
- Item count (60-90) from a test-prep site is unverified: https://link.psychometric-success.com/iready-test

**NWEA MAP Growth**
- Max items per math test: K-2 43, grades 2-5 43 (was 53), 6+ 43 (was 53) from July 2022. Test is untimed. Source: https://nwea.org/uploads/Average-MAP-Growth-Test-Durations.pdf
- Mean math duration (2017-18 data, before items were cut): Fall 2017 K 25.6 min, gr 1 29.2, gr 2 39.4, gr 3 47.6, gr 4 53.2, gr 5 57.5, gr 6 62.1, gr 7 64.2; 90th percentile in grade 1 was 45 min and in grade 2 was 61 min. Source: same PDF, Table 2.1. Winter 2018 means: gr 1 29.0, gr 2 40.7, gr 3 52.3. Spring: gr 1 43.1, gr 2 48.4.
- Current guidance: Iowa 2026-27 list says MAP Growth "Grades 1-2 (30-40 min.) Grades 3-6 (40-50 min.)". Source: https://educate.iowa.gov/media/11538/download
- 2025 norms: "nearly 14 million students, 30,000 schools, 7,000 districts, and all 50 states"; norms account for weeks of instruction. Source: https://www.nwea.org/blog/2025/whats-new-in-the-2025-map-growth-norms/

**IXL Diagnostic**
- LevelUp Benchmark: "Most students will finish in about 45 minutes, but the test has a one-hour time limit", 3 minutes per question limit, may be taken over multiple settings, auto-pauses when the student leaves the browser; students can use scratch paper and a "blue pencil icon" to write directly on screen. Source: https://www.ixl.com/materials/i_guides/Teacher_Guide_IXL_LevelUp_Math_Benchmark.pdf

- IXL LevelUp Universal Math Screener 10-20 min; Diagnostic 45 min (Iowa list): https://educate.iowa.gov/media/11538/download

Sources: https://psychometric-success.com/aptitude-tests/school-assessment-tests/renaissance-star-testing and https://www-mca.stjohns.k12.fl.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/PM3-Parent-Lettter-STAR-K-2.pdf. **BC FSA numeracy**
- Five parts across the fall of grade 4 and 7: paper numeracy task (3 written response questions and 1 self-reflection question, 45 min), online numeracy task (30 questions, 60 min), plus literacy components and a 5-min self-reflection. ### 1.3 Pattern across these products (for the design)

- Vendor adaptive tests that give national percentiles run 15-75 min and use up to 43 items (MAP Growth) or about 34 (Star, unverified); the i-Ready item count is unknown. Star (15-30 min) is the shortest of the norm-referenced group.
- Products aimed at parents, not schools (Kumon, Mathnasium, Beast Academy, Prodigy) give grade-level or starting-level output, not a percentile, and almost none publish an item count.
- No consumer product in this set shows a percentile without a national norm sample behind it. The three that show percentiles (i-Ready, MAP, Star) have norm samples in the millions. IXL claims national norms but the method was not in the fetched sources.

## 2. Recommended test length by age, and items per strand

### 2.1 What the sources say about attention and test duration

- There is no research-backed "minutes per year of age" rule. The common "2 to 3 minutes per year of age" appears only on parenting and education blogs, with no traceable study, and different sites give different numbers for the same age (for example 12-18 min vs 12-30 min for a 6-year-old). Treat it as folklore, not evidence. Examples: https://www.earlyyears.tv/attention-span-development/ and https://selfsufficientkids.com/?p=22241 (both unsourced; unverified).
- The "10-15 minute lecture attention" claim is also traced to one old report that other papers cite without primary data (Bradbury 2016, adult lectures). It does not apply to children. Source: https://epale.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/advan.00109.2016.pdf
- Developmental data (small): Betts, McKay, Maruff and Anderson (2006), 57 children aged 5-12, found rapid growth in sustained attention from ages 5-6 to 8-9 and a plateau from 8-9 to 11-12; performance was poorer on high-load tasks at every age. Source: https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/282136-the-development-of-sustained-attention-in-children--the-effect-of-age-and-task-load . A 2025 study of 479 people aged 6-17 found sustained attention changed with age into adolescence, and impulsivity changed only between ages 6 and 12 (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1609537/pdf, abstract only checked). This supports shorter sittings for ages 6-8 and more tolerance by 11-12, but no source gives a specific number of minutes.
- Fatigue and breaks (Danish national tests, about 2 million tests, children aged 8-15): scores fell about 0.9% of a standard deviation per hour later in the day, and a 20-30 min break before the test raised scores by 1.7% of an SD; low performers were affected more (break +2.7% SD). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790980 . This is about time of day, not test length, so it supports "schedule the test fresh, offer a break", not a minute count.
- Disengagement: NWEA flags a "rapid guess" when a response is much faster than the item's usual time (Wise: under 10% of average item time, max 10 seconds) and calls a student disengaged after three rapid guesses in a row. A Hechinger report on Wise's analysis of about 9 million grade 2-8 MAP tests says rapid guessing was higher on items with more reading. Sources: https://nwea.org/blog/?p=24854 and https://hechingerreport.org/?p=56835 (secondary summary of the Wise report; grade-by-grade rates not found, unverified).

### 2.3 Minimum items per strand for a reliable strength/weakness call

Evidence:
- Sinharay (2010), as reported in a peer-reviewed review: subscores based on fewer than 10 items almost never add value over the total score; subscores need "at least about 20 items" to have a hope of adding value; average operational subtest reliability was 0.38 for subtests averaging 19 items. Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00305/full (secondary citation of Sinharay; the same page shows two 34-item university exams whose two subscores had reliability 0.52-0.58 and added no value).
- Benton (2012, Cambridge Assessment): for a 180-mark test, "little point presenting subscores based on less than 30 marks"; in practice 50 or 60 marks may be needed. Across 94 subtests in 25 tests only 16 added value, and "no instances were found of subtests with less than 20 marks adding value". Subtests ranged from 11 to 69 marks. Source: https://www.cambridgeassessment.org.uk/Images/181088-calculating-the-number-of-marks-needed-in-a-subtest-to-make-reporting-subscores-worthwhile.pdf (GCSE exams, so a different population from young children; the principle transfers, the exact numbers do not).
- Why (Benton, same source): sub-skills within one subject tend to be strongly correlated, so the total score is informative about each sub-skill (GCSE science true-score correlations were about 0.9). I did not find a correlation figure for primary-school math strands.
- Practice in products: Khan Academy Mastery Challenge uses 2 questions per skill (6 questions, 3 skills) and only moves the skill up or down one level per session; a split result changes nothing. So it relies on repeated sessions, not one session, for a reliable call. Source: https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037494231 . Prodigy and IXL report strand breakdowns but do not publish items per strand (unverified).
- No source gives one universal "N items per skill for mastery" number. Slide-deck guidance of "at least four items per skill" exists but is classroom folklore (https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/norm-referenced-and-criterion-referencedpptx/259236463; unverified).

My illustrative calculations (not from a source; run with Python stdlib):
- Wilson 95% interval for the true accuracy behind an observed score: 4 of 6 correct gives 30%-90%; 5 of 6 gives 44%-97%; 6 of 8 gives 41%-93%; 7 of 10 gives 40%-89%; 9 of 12 gives 47%-91%; 14 of 20 gives 48%-85%. A strand with 6-8 items cannot separate "70%" from "50%".
- Spearman-Brown, assuming a 40-item total-test alpha of 0.88 (an assumption, not data): the same alpha implies a 6-item strand reliability of about 0.52, 8 items about 0.59, 10 items about 0.65, 12 items about 0.69. Total reliability for 24 items would be about 0.81 and for 36 items about 0.87 under the same assumption.
- So: the overall level is the reliable output; a single-sitting strand call with fewer than about 12 items is a hint, not a diagnosis.

### 2.4 Concrete recommendation (fixed-form core plus one routed block)

These assume two parts with a short break, a 2-3 item unscored warm-up, and the rule that no part is longer than the longest block that large-scale tests give to that age.

| Grade band | Active time (scored) | Scored items | Parts | Reported strands x items | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Grades 1-2 (ages 6-8) | 20-25 min | 24 (plus 3 unscored warm-up) | 2 parts of about 12 min, break of 2+ min or a second day within 7 days | 3 domains x 8 items | MAP K-2 form is capped at 43 items and averages 29-39 min in grades 1-2; schools plan 15-40 min for K-2 adaptive tests. 24 items at 0.7-0.9 min each is 17-22 min. Eight items per domain is still below the 10-item floor, so label domain results "early signal", and give the overall level as the main result. |
| (b) Grades 3-5 (ages 8-11) | 30-36 min | 36 | 2 parts of 15-18 min | 4 strands x 9 items | Each part is about half of the 30 min stage used in EQAO Grade 3 and the 25-30 min NAEP grade 4 blocks, with a break between parts. 36 items at about 0.9-1.0 min per item. FSA uses 4 content areas for these grades, so 4 strands align with BC reporting. |
| (c) Grades 6-7 (ages 11-13) | 40-45 min | 42 | 2 parts of 20-22 min | 4 strands x 10-11 items | 43 is the current MAP maximum for grades 6+. Two blocks of about 20-22 min are under the 30 min block used in NAEP digital and EQAO. |

Rules to carry with the numbers:
- Do not claim strand-level strengths or weaknesses from fewer than about 10 items. For all grades show strand results as three levels ("secure", "developing", "needs attention") with a visible "based on 8-9 questions" note, computed as in section 6.2 step 6, and show the overall working level as the headline.
- Make the strand label depend on both the score and the error pattern (for example, the same wrong answer on two items), which uses the diagnostic distractors in section 4 and does not require more items.
- Let each strand accumulate: the product already plans to adapt after every workbook, so treat the placement strand result as a prior that later workbook scans update (same approach as the Khan Mastery Challenge, repeated sessions).
- Offer the second part later the same day or within 7 days for grades 1-2. Place the test in the morning if the parent can choose (Danish data above: later hours cost 0.9% SD per hour; a 20-30 min break helped).
- Add an end-of-test question to the parent, "Did anyone help with answers?", and a response-time check for rapid guessing (under 10% of item median time, max 10 s). Three in a row flags a disengaged run, as NWEA does.

## 3. Question formats that work with mouse, keyboard and touch

### 3.1 Evidence on how young children handle input

- Ontario EQAO Grade 3 online math (children aged about 8) uses single selection, multiple selection, drag and drop, drop-down menu and checklist items, with a calculator, zoom, text-to-speech and high-contrast for all students, a 22-question sample test, released questions and an in-class platform introduction before the real test. Source: https://eqao.com/primary-division
- BC FSA online items (grades 4 and 7): multiple choice, drag-and-drop, drop-down menus, numerical value entry and placing items in sequence; question types may also include matching, scale, drawing, spatial/visual responses and interactive graphing. Content weighting: Number and Computational Fluency 35-45%, Patterns 20-25%, Geometry and Measurement 20-25%, Data and Probability 15-25%. Source: https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/education/administration/kindergarten-to-grade-12/assessment/fsa_description_and_specs.pdf
- Cognitive interviews with Australian Year 3 and Year 5 students on technology-enhanced numeracy items (ACARA / University of Canberra, 2014):
  - Year 3 students could not find the multiplication or division symbol on the keyboard to type answers; the report recommends giving +, -, x, and / symbols as click or drag tiles.
  - "Use Drawing Tool" items were hard for most students; some paper items "may not necessarily work well with a mouse on the screen".
  - Students preferred a click-to-create design over dragging objects into a box. A drop box that could not hold all the items confused students (balloons "disappeared"); keys and legends were often missed unless large and bold.
  - Partitioning a number line by hand: on the one-third item most Year 3 students who tried could not partition accurately, and on the 4-digit point item none who tried could, so free partitioning is a poor Year 3 item.
  - Recommends practice questions on the tools before the test, because digital items add "more mental and visual processing".
  - Source: https://www.nap.edu.au/_resources/2014_NASOP_Technically_Enhanced_Items_Research_Report_UC_Numeracy.pdf
- Touch accuracy: in studies with about 70 adults and children aged 7 and up, children missed on-screen touch targets far more often than adults, and the youngest group (7-10) found even the second-smallest ("small") targets harder than older groups did. Everyone struggled most with the smallest targets, and targets set away from the screen edge (with edge padding) were missed nearly twice as often as targets flush to the edge. Pixel sizes are device-specific and not restated here. Source: https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/anthony-et-al-jpuc2013.pdf
- Drag and drop: children now expect drag and drop in preference to point-and-click (CHI EA 2011 pilot), but a literature review notes that children have trouble holding pressure on a mouse for dragging and make more errors when dragging than pointing; touch-drag problems for children under 5 are common, and a tap-then-tap alternative is the usual advice. Sources: https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/children-may-expect-drag-and-drop-instead-of-point-and-click/ and https://cs.ubc.ca/wiki/do/view/Main/C-TOCLiteratureReview (the "tap-and-tap" guideline came through a search summary; age cut-off unverified for ages 6-8).
- Nielsen Norman Group, usability studies of children aged 3-12: kids preferred touchscreens and trackpads over a mouse until about age 9; slow typing is a limitation; text of 14 pt for young children and 12 pt for older children; children skip long instructions. Source: https://nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues
- Typing: no research norm by grade exists. Common Core expects "a minimum of one page in a single sitting" only from grade 4 (CCSS W.4.6; https://www2.cde.ca.gov/cacs/id/web/5718), and the NAEP writing framework cited limited keyboarding proficiency among elementary students as a reason grade 4 stayed on paper in 2011 (https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/pubs/writing/2015119.aspx and https://www.edweek.org/ew/articles/2007/03/14/27nagb.h26.html). One Australian online-writing study reported a median 9 words per minute for Year 3 (secondary; unverified). One Illinois district sets no typing speed target in grades 1-2 and 15-20 WPM in grade 3 (https://www.district30.org/departments/technology-and-innovation/keyboarding; local expectation, not a norm).
- Interface rules matter more for young children: in an AAAS study of more than 33,000 students in grades 4-12, elementary and middle school students scored lower on the computer system that did not let them go back to answered or skipped questions and made them press a separate button to mark an answer; high schoolers did not differ. Source: https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/navigation-of-computer-based-tests-matters-for-young-students-study-finds/2017/04
- Mode effect: in the first year of PARCC in Massachusetts (grades 3-8), online test takers scored about 0.10 SD lower in math (0.25 SD in ELA) than paper takers; the math gap fell to about a third in year 2. Source: https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/scores-were-lower-when-mass-students-took-parcc-exams-on-computers-study-finds/2019/01 (grade 3-only results not available). Implication: the first online test of a child who is new to the interface under-reads ability; add practice items.

### 3.2 Read-aloud audio

- IXL: questions at grade 2 or below can include audio; optional audio for math in grades 3-8 (2021 blog; IXL renamed its diagnostics in 2025). Source: https://blog.ixl.com/2021/01/28/common-questions-about-the-ixl-real-time-diagnostic/ . LevelUp math offers English audio and full Spanish support with audio. Source: https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/Benchmark_vs_Diagnostic_Comparison.pdf
- easyCBM: every math item has a read-aloud option (started by a sound test); items are written with few non-essential words; the read-aloud does not describe pictures or graphs. Source: https://district-help.easycbm.com/math-read-aloud-feature/
- EQAO makes text-to-speech available to all Grade 3 students. Source: https://eqao.com/primary-division
- Evidence on score effects: Buzick and Stone (2014), 19 studies, K-12: read-aloud raised scores by an average 0.56 SD in reading but only 0.13 SD in math, and was beneficial for students with disabilities. Source: https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/527e5ed2-1fd9-4abb-97fe-a9e7f93978d9 (summary page; full paper at https://doi.org/10.1111/emip.12040 not opened). The average math effect is small (0.13 SD), so the main value of audio is likely for ages 6-8 who read slowly and for English learners (design inference). Reading load raises disengagement: rapid guessing was higher on items with more reading (Hechinger summary of Wise, see 2.1).
- I found no randomized study of read-aloud in grade 1-2 digital math. Mark any claim about the size of the benefit at grade 1-2 as unverified.

### 3.3 Format guide by grade band

"Evidence" means a cited source above; "Design inference" means my reasoning from those sources, to be tested.

| Format | Grades 1-2 | Grades 3-5 | Grades 6-7 | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice, 3-4 large tap/click buttons (whole answer area is the target) | Use as default for recognition and concepts. Max 3 options for grade 1 | Default; 4 options | 4 options | Evidence: Anthony (touch misses), AAAS (direct click on answer, allow back). Design inference: 3 options at grade 1 |

Other interface rules supported above:
- Let the child go back, skip and change answers within a part (AAAS study; NN/g note that younger children do not use the browser Back button, so supply an on-screen Back).
- Use a minimum 14 pt text for grades 1-2 and 12 pt for older children (NN/g), and large targets (Anthony). Exact pixel sizes: unverified; test on a real tablet.
- Include a short unscored tutorial that practises each tool the test uses (Canberra, EQAO sample test).
- Per-item time cap with auto-advance is used by IXL (3 min) and should be generous; auto-pause when the child leaves the browser (IXL).
- Format mix matters for validity: keep at least half the items in grades 1-2 as tap or one-digit entry, so that typing is not what the test measures.

### 4.2 Tablet versus mouse for the scratchpad

- I found no study that reports scratchpad use rates split by tablet/stylus/finger versus mouse for children. Mark as unverified.
- Related facts: the NAEP scratchpad is described as usable "with a digital pencil or their fingers", and usage varies strongly between student groups (Digital Promise link above). In the EDM paper the scratchpad was not reported separately from the calculator and equation editor (link above). NN/g found children prefer touchscreens and trackpads over a mouse until about age 9 (link in 3.1), and the Canberra interviews found drawing tools hard with a mouse (link in 3.1).
- Design inference: expect near-zero spontaneous scratchpad use on mouse/trackpad, and measurable use on tablets. Log it as a feature (used / not used, stroke count, time on item, device type) so a later release can use real data.

### 4.3 Automatic analysis of handwritten work

- None of the evidence found shows reliable automatic scoring of young children's handwritten work:
  - DrawEduMath (NAACL 2025): 2,030 images of K-12 students' hand-drawn math responses and 11,661 teacher-written Q&A pairs; "state-of-the-art VLMs have considerable room to improve". A follow-up (BEA 2026) found 11 models over a year all underperform on work from students who need more pedagogical help, and struggle most with questions about assessing student error. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14877 (the BEA follow-up came through a search summary, not opened).
  - VEHME (EMNLP 2025) grades open-form handwritten math and approaches proprietary models on its test sets; the abstract gives no K-12 accuracy figures. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22798
  - Grading photographed handwritten university work with vision LLMs: most errors were transcription failures, plus hallucinated content and mishandled equivalent expressions (higher education, not children). Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19043 (via search summary; not opened).
  - Step-checking pipeline (recognise, then verify each line with a computer algebra system): step-marking F1 rose from 69.41 to 95.95 on addition/subtraction and from 61.45 to 89.95 on multiplication, using a digitally generated dataset of 6,000 handwritten derivations (synthetic, not children). Source: https://itc.ktu.lt/index.php/ITC/article/view/32066/15774 (abstract checked).
  - MathGPT.ai announced a step-by-step grader for handwritten paper work in July 2026 (higher education, vendor claim). Source: https://www.webull.com/news/15312419678659584
- Commercial K-12 recognition: Mathspace (above). No independent accuracy data found for any K-12 product.
- Implication: use AI reading of photos only as a hint to a parent ("we saw a regrouping error") with confirm/deny, never as the score. Unverified for ages 6-8.

### 4.4 Options for this product

- The score should come from structured answers (numeric entry, multiple choice with coded distractors), not from reading handwriting.
- Offer an optional on-screen scratchpad for grades 3-7 on tablet and a prompt "use paper if you like" for all; keep scratch paper allowed, as IXL does.
- If the brand stays paper-first (D-042 says an online test "can be gamed and does not match the paper format"), a hybrid is possible: structured answers on screen, plus one or two "explain it on paper" items per part that the parent photographs. These items are not scored on the first release; they feed the workbook as error evidence. This is a design inference.

## 5. Honest percentiles: can a new company show "percentile among same-grade children"?

Short answer: no, not honestly, at launch. A percentile is a statement about a defined reference group. Without a norm sample of the same test, the company has no reference group. The honest options are criterion-referenced statements, a grade-level (working-level) statement, and a clearly labelled internal comparison once enough children have taken the test.

### 5.1 How the big products get percentiles

- i-Ready: percentile ranks vs "post-pandemic national norms" built from data of more than 5 million students, same grade and testing season. Source: https://www.curriculumassociates.com/reviews/assessment/science-behind-the-diagnostic
- NWEA MAP Growth 2025 norms: "nearly 14 million students, 30,000 schools, 7,000 districts, and all 50 states"; norms are tied to weeks of instruction. Source: https://www.nwea.org/blog/2025/whats-new-in-the-2025-map-growth-norms/ . NWEA notes that the national distribution shifted and the same RIT score now maps to a higher percentile than under 2020 norms (a fifth-grade reading score at the 50th percentile under 2020 norms is now at the 56th). Same source.
- Star: percentile rank = percent of students nationally with equal or lower scores (Renaissance score definitions; secondary link in section 1).
- IXL: "nationally normed K-12 math and ELA"; method not found in the pages fetched. Source: https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/Benchmark_vs_Diagnostic_Comparison.pdf
- Consumer or tutoring products (Kumon, Mathnasium, Beast Academy, Prodigy, Zearn, DreamBox, Khan) report starting level, below/at/above grade level, or skill states. I found no published percentile from any of them (unverified for Kumon and Mathnasium reports, which are not public).

### 5.3 Could any of it support an honest comparison?

- Only as context, not as a percentile. A parent-facing line such as "In fall 2025, 59% of BC grade 7 students who wrote the FSA were On Track or Extending in numeracy" is a true third-party fact. It would not tell the parent how their child compares, and it would read as a link to our result unless the report says the FSA is a different test. A defensible use is a footnote in the report, not a comparison.
- A true link needs a linking study: give a sample of children both our assessment and the FSA (or another normed test, or FSA released items) and publish the cut-score alignment. This is possible later; cost and permission not researched (unverified).
- The repo already treats performance claims as binding: DESIGN.md says "No proof we do not have ... outcome percentages" and cites Competition Act s.74.01(1)(b), which requires an "adequate and proper test" before a performance claim (Competition Bureau guidance: https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/node/47 , via search summary). A percentile with no norm group would have no such basis behind it; ask counsel before using one. This is not legal advice.

| Option | What the parent sees | Needs | Honest? |

| A. ### 5.5 How many takers before an internal percentile is meaningful

Published guidance (norming literature):
- Conventional norming: "subsample sizes of n = 250 to n = 500 are recommended to generate norms with sufficiently high precision" (Lenhard and Lenhard 2021, citing Eid and Schmidt 2014 and Oosterhuis et al. 2016). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10621686
- Regression-based or continuous norming (one smooth model across grades): in that paper's simulation, with 100 persons per year (total N = 700), the error was 1.25 T-score points, and conventional norming needed four times the sample size to reach the same error. Same source (authors' own method). A software vendor states n = 100 per grade matches conventional n = 400 or more: https://psychometrica.de/cNorm_preparation_en.html (vendor claim).
- Oosterhuis, van der Ark and Sijtsma (2016): regression-based norming needs 2.5 to 5.5 times smaller samples; for a 50-item right/wrong test, holding the 75th percentile to within about one score point needs more than 10,000 with traditional norming and about 1,000 with regression-based norming; the tails (extreme percentiles) need more. Source: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13717320/Sample_Size_Requirements_for_Traditional_and_Regression_Based_Norms.pdf
- European (EFPA) guidance rates a representative norm group of more than 1,000 as "excellent" (secondary; same search summary as above; not verified at the EFPA text).
- The AERA/APA/NCME Standards (5.8, 5.9 as summarised in course slides) say norms should describe the population, sampling procedure, participation rates, weighting and test dates: https://marklhc.quarto.pub/psyc520-2025spring/slides/02-norms_and_standardized_scores.html (secondary; the 2014 Standards text was not checked).

My calculation (binomial approximation, near the median): the 95% interval on a percentile rank is about +/- 9.8 points at n = 100, +/- 6.9 at n = 200, +/- 4.9 at n = 400, +/- 3.1 at n = 1,000, and +/- 2.2 at n = 2,000. Near the 10th or 90th percentile the interval is about 60% as wide. This only counts sampling error; it does not count bias from who chooses to take the test.

Practical thresholds (design inference):
- Fewer than 100 test takers in a grade: show no comparison.
- About 100-250 per grade: fit one smooth model across grades (regression-based norming with grade and months of schooling) and show only broad bands ("lower third / middle third / upper third") with the n shown. A one-grade-at-a-time percentile at this size has +/- 7 to 10 points of noise.
- About 400-500 per grade: a numeric percentile per grade is defensible as an internal comparison, with its sample label.
- Before any use of "same-grade children": a representative or weighted sample (by region, school type, grade, time of year) or a linking study.
- Time of year matters: NWEA ties its norms to weeks of instruction, so internal norms need grade plus month in the model.
- Bias warning: families who choose a paid math workbook are not a random sample of BC families; the direction of bias is unknown (unverified).

## 6. Adaptive design without calibration data

### 6.1 How products start

- Prodigy: the placement test starts below the selected grade and moves up to one grade above, adapting to find strengths and gaps. Source: https://www.prodigygame.com/main-en/blog/prodigy-placement-test and https://prodigygame.com/blog/prodigy-grade-override-tool
- Khan Academy Mastery Challenge: fixed 6 questions (3 skills, 2 each); 2 of 2 correct moves a skill up a level, 0 of 2 moves it down, 1 of 2 holds. Source: https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037494231
- IXL diagnostic: the child's level is a range that narrows with answers until it becomes one number; "I don't know this yet" skips an unlearned topic. Source: https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/i_guides/Teacher_Plan_Diagnostic
- i-Ready: "specific test flows, stopping rules" that vary by grade band in math. Source: https://www.curriculumassociates.com/reviews/assessment/science-behind-the-diagnostic
- DreamBox: initial placement by the child's grade, then continuous adaptation across multiple grade levels; teachers may lower the start for children two or more grades behind. Source: https://help.kalamazoopublicschools.com/support/solutions/articles/3000104324-changing-dreambox-levels (secondary).
- Beast Academy: non-adaptive; a fixed test per unit, with the parent moving up or down a unit by the printed recommended score. Source: https://help.beastacademy.com/a/1861864-how-to-determine-your-student-s-level
- Individually given achievement tests use basal and ceiling rules: begin below the suspected level, continue until a run of consecutive correct answers establishes the basal and stop after a run of consecutive misses (ceiling); exact counts vary by test (not verified for the current Woodcock-Johnson). Source: https://info.riversideinsights.com/hubfs/Basal%20Ceiling%20Slides%20(1).pdf?hsLang=en
- Math Garden (Netherlands): an Elo-based item response model updates ability and difficulty estimates after every answered item, "allowing for on the fly item calibration"; scoring uses accuracy and response time; items are sampled for a mean success probability of about 0.75; 3,648 children completed more than 3.5 million problems in ten months. Source: Klinkenberg, Straatemeier and van der Maas (2011), Computers and Education 57(2), https://dare.uva.nl/id/de65bb65-d198-4dbc-ac7b-bc25ca23e627 (abstract). A later paper notes that a fixed K value trades stability against tracking change: https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/balancing-stability-and-flexibility-investigating-a-dynamic-k-val/ (search summary).
- Item calibration needs data: Linacre's table gives 27-61 well-targeted persons (50 suggested) for 99% confidence that an item's difficulty is within 1 logit, and 108-243 persons (about 150 suggested; more for high-stakes use) for within 0.5 logit; "30 examinees is enough for well-designed pilot studies". Source: https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm
- Multistage testing: calibration is harder when different children see different items. A simulation for WIDA ACCESS Online concluded that "a sample size of 3000 might be sufficient to achieve steady FT parameter estimates and item fit" (field-test folders in a multistage adaptive test). Source: https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/Research-Report-Impact-Ability-Range-Restriction-Item-Characteristics-ACCESS-Multistage-Adaptive-Testing.pdf . A small team cannot meet that at launch, so use hand-set cut points first.

### 6.2 A simple rule set a small team can build

Assumption: no item statistics. Difficulty comes from the BC curriculum grade tag plus a teacher-assigned tier. All numbers below are starting values to tune; they are design inference, not from a source.

1. Build the item bank by level. Make "Level g" sets for g = K to 8 (K and 8 are probes for Grade 1 and Grade 7). Each Level g set has 3-4 strands (grades 1-2: 3, grades 3-7: 4) and 8-11 items per strand with a tier tag: A (easy), B (typical), C (stretch). Tag source: the BC curriculum learning standard grade for the item, reviewed by two teachers. Pool size is about 9 levels x 32-44 items = about 290-400 items. Use parameterised numbers (several variants per item) so repeat attempts and shared answers do less harm.
2. Stage 1, "core" (about 50% of the test). Give items from the child's enrolled grade: 1/3 tier A, 1/2 tier B, 1/6 tier C, spread over the strands in blueprint proportions (FSA weights for Number, Patterns, Geometry and Measurement, Data and Probability in section 3). Grades 1-2: 12 items. Grades 3-5: 18 items. Grades 6-7: 21 items.
3. Route on core number-correct (%): 
   - 80% or more: "Up" form for Stage 2: half Level g+1 (tiers A-B), half Level g tier C. 
   - 40% to 79%: "On" form: Level g tiers B-C and Level g-1 tier B (about 3:1).
   - Under 40%: "Down" form: Level g-1 (60%) and Level g-2 (40%), tiers A-B. For Grade 1, "down" uses kindergarten-level items (counting, comparing, number bonds to 10).
4. Strand probe inside Stage 2 (keeps total length fixed): if the child got 0 of the first 2-3 items in a strand in the core, swap that strand's Stage 2 items to Level g-1. If 3 of 3, swap to Level g+1. 
5. Stopping and break: the fixed-length plan in section 2.4. Stop early (end the test with a "we have enough" screen) if a child answers 5 items in a row wrong in Stage 2 "Down" or leaves 3 items blank in a row; mark the result "low confidence". Flag disengagement by rapid guessing (see 2.4).
6. Working-level estimate: for each Level the child saw with at least 6 items, compute % correct. Working level = the highest Level with 70% or more correct; if no Level reaches 70%, the working level is one below the lowest Level seen and marked low confidence. Strand labels use only the items at the working level and the level one below (for children routed On or Up this is nearly all items, about 8 per strand in grades 1-2): "secure" = 75% or more correct, "developing" = 50-74%, "needs attention" = under 50%, each shown with its question count and the word "early signal". If a strand has fewer than 6 such items (common for children routed Down), show no label for that strand and say the test could not tell yet.
7. Guessing: multiple choice has a guess floor (25% with 4 options, 33% with 3). In grades 3-7 make at least a third of Stage 2 numeric entry; in grades 1-2 use 1-2 digit numeric entry where the content allows (section 3.3). Count every item once, and do not apply a guessing correction until data exist. The 70% working-level bar sits above the guess floor for both option counts.
8. After launch, calibrate on the fly: 
   - Log every response (item, answer, correct, response time, device, part, date, enrolled grade, age). 
   - At 50 responses per item, set the tier from the percent correct among children at the item's own grade: A above 80%, B 50-80%, C below 50% (hand rule). Flag items under 20% or above 95% correct, and items whose correctness has a low correlation with the total score, for rewrite. 
   - At about 150-250 responses per item (Linacre figure above, 0.5 logit), fit a one-parameter (Rasch) model and replace the hand cut points with ability estimates. Treat 3,000 per field-test folder (WIDA) as the level for stable multistage routing parameters. 
   - Optionally switch to Elo-type updating (Math Garden approach) for on-the-fly difficulty updates.
   - Re-set routing cut points (80% and 40%) from the data so that about one in five children go Up and one in five go Down (a target, not a rule from a source).
9. Release gate: before any of this goes live, run 20-30 children per grade band in a pilot with a person watching (Linacre: 30 persons show what is happening). Check: finishing time, items skipped, routing balance, tool usage by device, and whether the working level agrees with the teacher's or parent's view of the child.

### 6.3 What this rule set cannot do

- It cannot give a precise ability score. It gives a working level and three-level strand labels.
- Routing on a short core is noisy (a 12-item core has the same wide intervals as in section 2.3). The strand probe and the "low confidence" flag exist to limit harm, not to remove it.
- It gives no percentile. See section 5.

## Recommendations

- Total length per grade band (active time, two parts with a break; add a 2-3 item unscored warm-up):
  - Grades 1-2 (ages 6-8): 20-25 min, 24 scored items, two parts of about 12 min; allow part two on another day within 7 days.
  - Grades 3-5 (ages 8-11): 30-36 min, 36 items, two parts of 15-18 min (under the 30 min block that EQAO and NAEP use).
  - Grades 6-7 (ages 11-13): 40-45 min, 42 items, two parts of 20-22 min (43 items is the current MAP maximum).
  - Why: operational tests give 15-40 min to grades K-2 (Star 15-25, i-Ready K-1 25-35, MAP grades 1-2 30-40) and 25-30 min blocks to grades 3-4 (EQAO, NAEP); no source gives a minutes-per-age rule, so do not cite one.
- Items per strand: 3 domains x 8 items (grades 1-2), 4 strands x 9 items (grades 3-5), 4 strands x 10-11 items (grades 6-7). Use the four FSA content areas for grades 3-7 (Number and Computational Fluency, Patterns, Geometry and Measurement, Data and Probability).
  - These are below the roughly 20 items at which the literature finds subscores add value over the total (Sinharay 2010; Benton 2012). So headline the overall working level, show strands as three levels with "based on N questions", and let strand evidence accumulate across workbooks.
- Formats to use: multiple choice with 3 (grade 1) or 4 large targets; numeric entry on an on-screen number pad (max 2 digits in grades 1-2, 4 digits in grades 3-5) with operation tiles; tap-then-tap or click-to-place instead of free drag; number line with pre-marked ticks; money by tapping coins; clock by multiple choice; audio on by default for grades 1-2; text at 14 pt or larger; back, skip and change allowed; one unscored tutorial per tool. Do not score free drawing or free number-line partitioning.
- Show-your-work approach: score only structured answers. Use multiple choice distractors coded to named misconceptions for diagnosis. Offer an optional on-screen scratchpad for grades 3-7 and say "use paper if you like"; log scratchpad use and device. Treat photographed paper work as unscored evidence for workbook planning, and do not auto-score handwriting (no K-12 accuracy data; DrawEduMath shows models still struggle with student error).
- Honest comparison approach: report "at / below / above BC grade-level expectations" per strand and a working level, with a short method note and teacher-set cut scores. Do not use "percentile" or "same-grade children" until a representative norm or linking study exists. Add an internal comparison only after at least 100 takers per grade (broad bands, one smooth model across grades) and 400-500 per grade (numeric), labelled "children who took this assessment (n = ...)". Use the FSA only as a footnote about a different test; do not reuse the Ministry's level names for our result.
- Adaptive approach: a two-stage routed test with hand-set rules. Stage 1 gives the enrolled-grade core (12 / 18 / 21 items); route at 80% or more to an "up" form, 40-79% to "on", under 40% to "down"; add a strand probe (0 of first 2-3 items goes down a level, 3 of 3 goes up). Working level = highest level with 70% or more correct on at least 6 items; flag low confidence. Pilot with 20-30 children per band with an observer, then re-tier items from data at 50 responses per item and fit a Rasch model at 150-250 responses per item.
- Before building: decide how the paper-first decision D-042 and an online test fit together (a hybrid with photographed "explain it on paper" items is one option), and add a "did anyone help?" question plus rapid-guess flags so that parent help and disengagement are visible in the result.
