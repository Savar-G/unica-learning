# Hero copy research (read-only research, 2026-10-09)

Purpose: inform a rewrite of the Pagewise hero (personalized paper math and reading workbooks, ages 6-12, mailed home).
Method: WebSearch and WebFetch. Live copy fetched 2026-10-09. Some sources were secondhand (noted). No code changed.

## Part 1. Expert rules (12)

1. **The litmus test: read only the headline - do you know what is sold?** Describe the product literally; no slogans. Source: Julian Shapiro, https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages
2. **Headline = the bad alternative, made better, as an action statement.** Find the value prop by naming what people do without you, then say how you beat it. Source: Julian Shapiro (same URL).
3. **Add one hook: a bold specific claim OR a pre-empted buying objection.** Do not bloat the header or answer every objection in it. Source: Julian Shapiro (same URL).
4. **Subhead = say exactly what it is + explain how the headline claim is possible.** One or two breezy sentences. Source: Julian Shapiro (same URL).
5. **Can you visualise it?** If the reader cannot picture it they will not remember it. "1,000 songs in your pocket" beats "32GB storage". Source: Harry Dry, https://gp.demandcurve.com/lessons/fundamental-rules-of-good-copy
6. **Can you falsify it?** A claim must be provable true or false. "Reads every day" passes, "smart" fails. Source: Harry Dry (same URL).
7. **Can nobody else say it?** Say something unique to you. Three yeses to the three tests = good copy; three noes = rubbish. Source: Harry Dry (same URL) and https://www.upgrow.io/harry-dry-copywriting-3-rules/
8. **Clear beats clever; write about 50 headlines first; start from formulas.** Useful formulas: "[Desired outcome] by [how]", "Helping [who] do [what]", "[Hard thing] in [time]", quantity + proof. The "Helping ___ do ___" form tells visitors they are in the right place. Source: Copyhackers / Joanna Wiebe, https://copyhackers.com/2022/07/headline-formulas/
9. **Front-load the key words.** Users scan the first words only; important keywords go first. Source: Jakob Nielsen (NN/g), secondhand via https://www.wyliecomm.com/web-page-headlines/ (direct nngroup.com fetches returned 404, so this is unverified at the primary source).
10. **Match the headline to the reader's awareness level.** Unaware / problem-aware / solution-aware / product-aware / most-aware. Early stages lead with the problem or desire; later stages lead with product, differentiator, or offer. Source: Eugene Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising (1966), via https://blog.adbeat.com/5-levels-customer-awareness/ and https://personalmba.com/levels-of-awareness/ (secondary summaries).
11. **Lead the hero with value, not features, and derive it in order.** Competitive alternatives -> unique attributes -> value -> target customer -> category. Headline carries the value; subhead names the unique attribute that makes it true. (The hero mapping is my inference from the framework.) Source: April Dunford, https://www.wudpecker.io/blog/obviously-awesome-the-guide-to-perfecting-your-product-positioning
12. **Align headline and button copy; use the survey of real customers to find the objection.** Ask buyers what nearly stopped them and ask non-buyers why they did not buy; the answer is the hook. Sources: Julian Shapiro (same URL); Wiebe via https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/copywriting-lessons-joanna-wiebe

Gaps: Nielsen Norman direct articles and Marketing Examples pages did not load (404/500). Schwartz and Dunford come from secondary summaries.

## Patterns

1. **Say literally what it is, then why it matters** (Shapiro litmus test). Seen: Stripe, Mailchimp, Brilliant. Fit: high, but we must say "workbook mailed to your child", not "learning platform".
2. **Specific, falsifiable number as the hook.** Superhuman ("Save 4+ hours each week"), Hims ("as fast as 3 months"), Synthesis ("less than $1/day"). Fit: medium. Use only numbers we can prove (copy rules in DESIGN.md forbid invented proof). A true specific: "one workbook, one child", page counts, mail cadence.
3. **Name the fear or bad alternative and remove it.** Stitch Fix ("No Risk"), Synthesis ("never have to worry about them falling behind"), Mailchimp ("minus the learning curve"). Fit: high. Our bad alternative is the one-size-fits-all grade workbook.
4. **Possessive / "your child" framing.** Synthesis ("Your child's personal math tutor"), Brilliant ("Your personal tutor"). Fit: highest. It makes the personalization instantly visible in the first words (front-loading).
5. **State the premise as a plain fact.** Kumon ("Every child learns math at a different pace."), the same belief we hold. Fit: high as subhead; it names the problem without a slogan and is easy to agree with.
6. **Name the mechanism in the subhead** (how the claim is possible). Stitch Fix lists size, style, budget; Brilliant gives credentials; Notion lists three verbs. Fit: high. Subhead should say the inputs (their level, pace, interests) and the output (a workbook made for them, mailed).
7. **The entry step is the CTA** (quiz or consultation). Stitch Fix "Take the quiz", Prose "Take your free consultation", Function of Beauty "Shop Your Hair Type". Fit: high, since our signup asks about the child. CTA should name what the parent does, not "Submit".
8. **Persona split at the top.** Brilliant ("I'm a learner / I'm a parent or teacher"), Prodigy. Fit: low. We sell to parents only; skip.
9. **Emotional, generic outcome with no proof** ("joy of learning through play", "fun & enriching"). Lovevery, KiwiCo, Prodigy. Anti-pattern: fails visualise, falsify, and nobody-else tests; avoid.
10. **Brand-fame or pun headlines** (Apple "Pro further.", Ritual "Watch Your Tone", Mathnasium wordplay). Anti-pattern for an unknown brand; Schwartz awareness level is low for us, so lead with the problem or the plain product, not cleverness.

Best fit for a personalized-for-one-child product: patterns 4 + 5 + 6 (possessive, plain-fact premise, mechanism in subhead), with 3 in the headline or subhead and 7 in the CTA. Use pattern 2 only with provable specifics. The paper, mailed, no-screen angle is a differentiator that passes "nobody else can say it" and is visualisable (a package on the doorstep); none of the 21 examples uses it.
